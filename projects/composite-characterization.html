MSE 586 · COMPOSITE CHARACTERIZATION

Composite Materials Characterization and Failure Analysis

Experimental Mechanics | Digital Image Correlation | Classical Laminate Theory | Failure Prediction

Across 14 experiments over 14 weeks in Purdue University’s MSE 586 composite characterization laboratory, I worked with a broad experimental program connecting composite manufacturing, mechanical characterization, laminate mechanics, and failure analysis. The work included prepreg processing and specimen preparation, mechanical testing, digital image correlation (DIC), optical microscopy, stress transformations, Classical Laminate Theory (CLT), and failure criteria. Responsibilities were shared across the course experiments; the work below highlights methods and findings from the program.

PROGRAM
Purdue University · MSE 586
SCOPE
14 experiments · 14 weeks
MATERIAL SYSTEM
IM7/8552 carbon-fiber/epoxy

Testing · DIC · Microscopy · CLT · Failure analysis
01 — MICROSTRUCTURE

How do processing and microstructure connect?

Optical microscopy and image segmentation were used to characterize the fiber and matrix regions of the composite. The microscopy-based fiber volume fraction was approximately 58%, providing a measured link between material architecture and the mechanical response observed in later experiments.

Prepreg layup and vacuum bagging were part of the manufacturing work. Together, processing quality and microstructure influence the reinforcement fraction, void content, and directional properties that characterize a laminate.

02 — OFF-AXIS RESPONSE

How does loading direction affect tensile response?

IM7/8552 off-axis specimens were tested in tension at 15°, 30°, 45°, 60°, and 75°. DIC provided full-field strain measurements that could be considered alongside applied stress, stress/strain tensor transformations, and Tsai–Wu failure predictions.

Experimental ultimate tensile strength decreased from approximately 346 MPa at 15° to 69 MPa at 75°. The measured apparent shear modulus G12 ranged from approximately 5.04 to 3.60 GPa across the tested orientations.

Apparent shear-modulus estimates can vary with nonuniform strain fields, boundary constraints, and measurement assumptions used to reduce the data. Extension–shear coupling and local strain variation mean the extracted value is not independent of specimen orientation and test interpretation.

03 — LAMINATE ARCHITECTURE

How does stacking sequence shape response?

Double-Double and cross-ply laminates were compared through their stacking sequences, directional stiffness, Poisson’s ratio, and failure morphology. The cross-ply laminates exhibited an axial modulus of approximately 75 GPa; the two tested Double-Double configurations exhibited approximately 40.4 and 55.6 GPa.

These values describe the particular layups tested, not all Double-Double laminates. CLT predictions provided a mechanics-based comparison, while Tsai–Wu first-ply failure predictions were considered against experimentally observed ultimate failure—a distinction between predicted onset and specimen-level failure.

04 — OPEN-HOLE TENSION

How do architecture and hole size affect notched strength?

Open-hole tensile testing followed ASTM D5766 methods across three laminate architectures, three nominal hole diameters, and 45 tensile coupons. DIC was used to measure strain fields around the notches and to support examination of failure morphology and notch sensitivity.

Measured notched strength retention decreased as nominal hole diameter increased in the plotted comparisons. The failure photographs show different damage patterns across laminate architectures, including differences in the extent and appearance of fracture around the hole; these observations are specific to the tested specimens.

05 — MODEL VALIDATION

Which analytical criterion best matches each architecture?

Point Stress Criterion (PSC), Average Stress Criterion (ASC), and Modified Point Stress Criterion (MPSC) predictions were compared with experimental strength-retention ratios. The lowest reported root-mean-square error (RMSE) varied by laminate: ASC for Laminate A (0.0462), MPSC for Laminate B (0.0286), and PSC for Laminate C (0.0722).

This variation shows that model performance depends on laminate architecture. Greater model complexity does not automatically improve predictive accuracy: a more complex criterion can fit one layup better while another criterion performs best for a different one.
